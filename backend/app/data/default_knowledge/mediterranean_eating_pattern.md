# Mediterranean-Style Eating Pattern -- Summary Reference

**Primary source:** *Scientific Report of the 2025 Dietary Guidelines
Advisory Committee*, Part D, Chapter 2: Dietary Patterns (U.S. Departments
of Agriculture and Health and Human Services, December 2024). Official
copy:
https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/Part%20D_Ch%202_Dietary%20Patterns_FINAL_508.pdf
(retrieved for this bundle August 20, 2026).

**License/provenance note:** The DGAC Scientific Report is a work of the
United States federal government (17 U.S.C. SS 105) and is not subject to
copyright in the United States. The graded conclusion statements below are
quoted from it directly, as public domain government material; the framing
and the "relevance to this app" section are Chef's own.

## What this file is, and what it deliberately is not

This is a **dietary** reference for meal planning. It describes an eating
pattern and the strength of the evidence behind it. It contains no
diagnostic thresholds, no lab-value targets and no treatment
recommendations, and nothing here should be retrieved and presented as
clinical guidance -- the same rule the rest of this bundle follows.

## What the evidence says, graded

The 2025 Advisory Committee's own conclusion for adults and older adults,
quoted in full, is the strongest single statement available in a public
domain source for why this pattern is worth planning meals around:

> "Dietary patterns consumed by adults and older adults that are
> characterized by higher intakes of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts,
> whole grains, unsaturated relative to saturated fats and lower sodium,
> and lower intakes of red and processed meat, refined grains, and
> sugar-sweetened foods and beverages are associated with lower risk of
> cardiovascular disease, including clinically meaningful improvements in
> blood lipids and blood pressure."

**Grade: Strong.** That is the Committee's highest evidence grade, and it
names blood lipids explicitly -- which is the outcome this household's
stated goal turns on.

The parallel conclusion for children and adolescents is graded **Moderate**:

> "Dietary patterns consumed by children and adolescents that are
> characterized by higher intakes of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts,
> whole grains, fish and/or seafood, and unsaturated fats and oils and
> lower intakes of red and processed meats and sugar-sweetened foods and
> beverages are associated with lower systolic and diastolic blood
> pressure and triglycerides later in life."

## What characterizes the pattern

**Eat more of:** vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains, fish and
seafood, and vegetable oils higher in unsaturated fat. Some healthy
patterns in this family also include low-fat or non-fat dairy and foods
lower in sodium.

**Eat less of:** red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened foods and
beverages, refined grains, and saturated fat.

Where the older 2015 Committee named a "Healthy Mediterranean-style
Dietary Pattern" as a distinct approach, the 2025 report treats
Mediterranean-style eating as one member of this broader family rather
than grading it separately. Popular descriptions of the Mediterranean diet
emphasise "fruits, vegetables, legumes, fish, olive oil, and nuts," which
is the same list with olive oil named outright.

**Two components stand out as consistent across nearly every outcome the
Committee reviewed:** seafood and nuts. The fat guidance is comparative
rather than absolute -- it is *unsaturated relative to saturated*, not
"low fat".

## An honest gap in this file

**There is no serving-count table here, and that is a limitation rather
than an editorial choice.** The companion `dash_eating_pattern.md` file
carries one because NHLBI publishes DASH as a quantified pattern. No
current, retrievable, public domain U.S. federal source quantifies a
Mediterranean pattern the same way: the 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines'
Appendix 3 did so, but that edition has been superseded by the 2025-2030
guidelines (see `dietary_guidelines_2025_2030.md`), and the superseding
edition does not appear to carry a replacement table.

So this file grounds *what* the pattern is and *how strong* the evidence
is, and leaves the per-day arithmetic to DASH and to the current Dietary
Guidelines. If an official quantified Mediterranean pattern is published
later, it belongs here and this section should be replaced by it.

## Relevance to this app's design

- The **"unsaturated relative to saturated"** framing is directly usable
  at generation time: it makes olive oil, nuts and seafood positive
  signals rather than merely permitted ones, which a generic "healthy"
  instruction does not.
- The **lower-intake list** (red and processed meat, refined grains,
  sugar-sweetened foods and beverages) overlaps almost exactly with what
  the bundled DASH file asks to limit, so a plan built to satisfy one is
  largely consistent with the other. Where they genuinely differ, DASH's
  own file says so rather than papering over it.
- **Processed meat appears on the lower-intake side of every version of
  this conclusion.** That pairs with the NOVA group captured on scanned
  inventory items: an item classified NOVA 4 is evidence for the same
  judgement this pattern is asking the planner to make.
- **Seafood and nuts are the two components to bias toward** when a slot
  is otherwise unconstrained, on the Committee's own reading of the
  evidence.
